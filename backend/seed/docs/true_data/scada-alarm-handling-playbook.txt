SCADA Alarm Handling Playbook

SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) systems continuously poll
remote terminal units at substations and report equipment status, analog
measurements (current, voltage, temperature), and discrete alarms to the
control center. Alarms are classified by severity P1 through P4, matching the
same severity scale used for outages, so operators can triage a mixed queue
of alarms and outage events consistently.

Alarm severity guidance:

- P1: immediate safety or reliability risk. Examples: differential protection
  trip, breaker failure, a substation bus undervoltage condition. Requires
  acknowledgement within 5 minutes and, in most cases, immediate dispatch.
- P2: significant equipment stress that could escalate. Examples: sustained
  overcurrent, transformer overtemperature, communication loss to an entire
  substation (which blinds the control center to that facility's status).
  Requires acknowledgement within 15 minutes.
- P3: localized or single-device issue that does not immediately threaten
  service. Examples: a single sensor reading out of calibration range, a
  non-critical breaker status discrepancy. Requires acknowledgement within
  one shift.
- P4: informational or low-priority. Examples: scheduled-maintenance mode
  entry/exit, minor voltage sag within tolerance. Reviewed during routine
  shift handoff.

Standard alarm workflow:

1. Alarm fires and appears in the SCADA alarm list with a timestamp, source
   equipment, alarm type, and severity.
2. The on-shift operator acknowledges the alarm (setting acked_by) within the
   severity-appropriate window above.
3. The operator determines whether the alarm is a nuisance/instrumentation
   issue or reflects real equipment behavior, cross-checking related
   measurements (for example, an overcurrent alarm should correlate with an
   actual load increase, not appear in isolation).
4. If real equipment action is required, the operator either issues a remote
   SCADA command (where the equipment supports remote control and it is safe
   to do so) or dispatches a field crew.
5. Once the underlying condition clears, the alarm is marked resolved. Alarms
   that remain unresolved across a shift change must be explicitly handed off
   with current status and any action already taken.

"Communication loss" alarms deserve special handling: they mean the control
center has lost visibility into that substation's real equipment state, not
that equipment has necessarily failed. Restoring communications (which may
require a field crew to power-cycle the RTU) should be treated with the same
urgency as a P2 alarm even though no equipment fault has been confirmed,
because the control center cannot safely operate blind equipment remotely.
