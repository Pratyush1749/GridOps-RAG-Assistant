Transformer Overload and Overtemperature Response

Power transformers are rated in MVA (megavolt-amperes) and are designed to
operate continuously at or below their nameplate capacity. Loading above
nameplate for extended periods accelerates insulation aging and, if sustained
long enough, can cause internal arcing, oil breakdown, or catastrophic
failure.

Common SCADA alarm types associated with transformer distress:

- "transformer overtemperature": top-oil or winding temperature has exceeded
  a configured threshold. A P2 alarm typically indicates elevated but
  survivable loading; a P1 alarm indicates the transformer is approaching a
  trip setpoint.
- "overcurrent": current on the transformer's low-side or high-side bushings
  has exceeded a percentage of nameplate rating, often correlated with peak
  demand periods (summer afternoons for air-conditioning load, winter
  mornings for heating load).
- "low oil level": the conservator or main tank oil level has dropped below
  the normal band, which can indicate a leak and reduces the transformer's
  cooling and dielectric margin.
- "differential protection trip": internal fault protection has detected a
  current imbalance between the transformer's windings, consistent with an
  internal short. This alarm should be treated as P1 and the transformer
  should not be re-energized without inspection.

Recommended response procedure:

1. On an overtemperature or overcurrent alarm, dispatch operations should
   check current loading against the transformer's nameplate MVA rating and
   compare against the same time last week to determine whether the load is
   abnormal or expected seasonal peak.
2. If loading exceeds 100% of nameplate and is expected to persist (for
   example during a heat event), initiate load transfer to a neighboring
   feeder/transformer where capacity allows, or request voluntary demand
   reduction from large commercial/industrial customers on that feeder.
3. On a low oil level alarm, schedule a field inspection within 24 hours;
   this is not an immediate trip risk but should not be deferred past one
   business day.
4. On a differential protection trip, do not attempt to re-energize the
   transformer remotely. Dispatch a crew for physical inspection before any
   switching.
5. Log every transformer alarm event, its severity, whether it was resolved
   automatically or required a crew action, and any load-transfer or
   de-rating decision made, for post-event trending and asset-health
   analysis.

Transformers with three or more overtemperature or overcurrent alarms within
a rolling 90-day window should be flagged for a capacity upgrade review,
since recurring overload alarms are a leading indicator of premature
transformer failure.
